Peloton Phase Transitions

Changing dynamics through speed

A pack of cyclists, a peloton, is a striking example of collective motion, easily observed in races like the Tour de France. Although team directors can instruct riders where to be in the pack, collective formations are largely determined by physics and riders' physiological limitations. Underlying formations are these basic rules: each rider seeks to save energy by riding behind others, to stay with the group, and to move up when they can. As the peloton moves through changing speeds, distinct phases can be observed. Press Play and watch the speed rise and fall and the collective dynamics that emerge.

How it works

Drafting

At racing speeds most of a cyclist's energy is spent pushing air aside. Drafting, or riding close behind other riders, saves a large share of it. With the Tracers button switched on, each rider in the animation trails a thin line showing its path over the last few seconds, seen from a viewpoint that moves at the pace of the pack. Riders holding a drafting position leave short traces; riders moving up the sides leave long traces behind them, and riders drifting back or losing contact leave traces that point forwards.

Limited energy

Every rider has a maximal sustainable output (MSO), the most power it can keep up. The riders here have MSOs derived from riders in a real women's elite provincial-level Scratch race on a velodrome, ranging from 215 to 337 watts (W). Some are stronger than others; the range for the current riders is shown in the corner of the animation. Riders are coloured by how hard they are working: green means they can still pass others (PCR below 0.74), amber means they must draft to maintain speed and cannot pass (PCR 0.74 to 1), and red means they cannot hold a wheel even when drafting (PCR above 1). Riders can go a little over their limit for a short while, then must recover.

Moving up

Riders with spare energy drift up the sides of the bunch and tuck back in further ahead; riders at the front take a turn in the wind, and then swing off or are passed by fresher riders. But passing means riding in the wind, faster than the bunch, and that costs energy riders may not have.

Observe this

Three graphs under the animation follow the pack. The braid draws each rider's place in the order as a coloured strand: every crossing is an overtake, so a mixing bunch weaves a dense braid and a single-file line is shown as parallel lines. The effort-versus-mixing graph plots PCR against Cx* once a second; the dashed lines are the thresholds that separate the phases, and the trail loops as the speed rises and falls. The phase strips record which phase appeared at each speed on the way up and on the way down.

Compact and static, or compact and mixing. At 30 km/h everyone has energy to spare. The bunch is compact, and riders hold position or swap places freely.

Surging. As the speed rises, effort goes up and the reshuffling continues: the bunch is accelerating and reorganising.

Strung out. Above about 41 km/h the weaker riders are close to their limits. They can follow a wheel but no longer pass, so the bunch narrows into a long single-file line.

Breaking apart. Push the speeds above about 45 km/h and even full drafting is not enough for the weakest riders: gaps open, and the bunch splits into groups.

Strung out and tired. Watch the speed come back down. At speeds where the bunch was surging on the way up, it now stays strung out: riders have used up their reserves and cannot move up. The two strips in the phase chart differ. The state of the bunch depends on its history as well as its speed: hysteresis.

The math

The phases are identified with two numbers, measured once a second.

Effort. The peloton convergence ratio compares the power a sheltered rider needs with what it can sustain:

PCR = Pfront dMSO

Pfront is the power the front rider needs at the current speed, and d (0.62 here) is the fraction of it a sheltered rider still needs. The value shown is the average over riders. Below about 0.49 riders have plenty to spare; above about 0.74 they can follow but not pass; above 1 they cannot hold a wheel even when drafting.

Mixing. Track each rider's place in the order as a strand in time, and every overtake becomes a crossing of two strands, as in a braid. Let X be the number of riders whose position changed in the last second, among N riders. Then the positional complexity is

Cx* = 1 + ln(1 + 12X / N)

The phase rules combine the two: low mixing with high PCR is strung out; high mixing with low PCR is compact and mixing; PCR above 1 is breaking apart; and whether PCR is rising or falling separates surging from easing off, and strung out from strung out and tiring.
